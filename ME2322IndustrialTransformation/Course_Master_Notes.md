## Lecture 0 — Intro Session: Course Overview & Exam Playbook

*Source: "ME2322 20261027 Intro v5 shared.pdf" — slides from the introductory lecture by Emrah Karakaya, 27 October 2026.*

### TL;DR

ME2322 studies **industrial transformation and technological innovation**: why industries are born, consolidate, and die; why some technologies conquer entire economies while others stay niche; and how firms, industries, and societies co-evolve with technology. The course has two **intended learning outcomes (ILOs)**: (1) describe, explain, and *use* concepts, models, and theories to analyze industrial dynamics and technological innovation — among other things applied to industrial development in Sweden; and (2) analyze the *two-way* relationship between societal challenges and industrial dynamics: challenges shape innovation, and innovation shapes how society responds to challenges.

The course is deliberately organized around **two societal challenges** — AI-driven transformation and sustainability transitions — and **four industries** (food, energy, gaming, health) covered by guest lectures. The theory lectures cover **both the supply side and the demand side** of technological innovation: L1 industrial dynamics (dominant design, general-purpose technologies), L2 socio-technical transitions (technological innovation systems, multi-level perspective), L3 diffusion of innovations (adopters, change agents), L4 critical perspectives on transitions. Assessment has two parts: **TEN1**, an individual oral exam taken three students at a time (2 ECTS), and **INL1**, a group assignment plus an individual reflection essay.

### Key Concepts & Vocabulary

- **Industrial dynamics** — the study of how industries change over time: entry and exit of firms, growth and decline, the evolution of market structure. "Industrial dynamics" is the L1 theme: dominant designs and GPTs explain *why* these dynamics happen.
- **Technological innovation** — new or significantly improved products, processes, or services. Say it out loud as: "Innovation is not invention — an invention only becomes an innovation when it reaches the market and is adopted."
- **Societal challenge** — a large, long-term, systemic problem that markets alone do not automatically solve (e.g., climate change, AI-driven transformation). In this course: *AI-driven transformation* and *sustainability transitions*.
- **Supply side vs. demand side of innovation** — the supply side asks who invents, produces, and commercializes technology (industries, firms, innovation systems); the demand side asks who adopts and uses it, and why (adopters, diffusion, users). A complete theory of innovation needs both.
- **Dominant design** (L1) — the one product architecture that wins the allegiance of the marketplace and becomes the industry standard. Full treatment in Module 1A.
- **General-purpose technology, GPT** (L1) — a technology that is pervasive, keeps improving, and spawns complementary innovations across many sectors (steam, electricity, ICT). Full treatment in Module 1B.
- **Technological innovation system, TIS** (L2) — the network of actors, institutions, and interactions that develops and diffuses a specific technology.
- **Multi-level perspective, MLP** (L2) — explains transitions as interactions among three levels: niche innovations, the incumbent socio-technical regime, and the external landscape.
- **Diffusion of innovations** (L3) — how new technologies spread through a population over time, and who adopts early vs. late (adopters, change agents).
- **Critical perspective** (L4) — questioning the hidden assumptions in transition/innovation research, e.g., who benefits, who is excluded, whether "sustainability" claims are real.
- **TEN1 / INL1** — KTH exam codes: TEN1 = the oral exam (2 ECTS); INL1 = the written assignment part (group report + reflection).

## Lecture 1 — Industrial Dynamics: Dominant Design & General-Purpose Technologies

*Readings: Utterback (1996), Ch. 2 — covered in Module 1A; Bresnahan (2024) — covered in Module 1B.*

L1 asks two macro questions about **industrial dynamics**: (1) *Why does a young, chaotic industry full of competing designs suddenly collapse into a single standard, and who survives that collapse?* — answered by Utterback's dominant-design theory. (2) *How does a technology become so general that it transforms the whole economy, and why hasn't AI done that yet?* — answered by Bresnahan's general-purpose technology (GPT) analysis. Together they give you the supply-side spine of the course: how technologies and industries co-evolve.

### Module 1A — Dominant Design and the Survival of Firms (Utterback, 1996)

*Source: J.M. Utterback, "Dominant Designs and the Survival of Firms," Chapter 2 of Mastering the Dynamics of Innovation (Harvard Business School Press, 1996), pp. 23–56. One of the two assigned readings for L1.*

#### TL;DR

New industries of assembled products begin in a **fluid phase**: many firms enter, producers and customers experiment together, and no firm has a "lock" on the market — nobody has perfected the product, mastered manufacturing, or controlled distribution. Then a **dominant design** emerges: one product architecture that "wins the allegiance of the marketplace" by combining features already proven in earlier product variants. From that moment the logic of competition flips: product innovation slows down, process innovation (cost, quality, scale) accelerates, and the number of firms collapses in an **exit wave** ("shakeout") until a few large firms remain. The chapter's core claim: **the emergence of the dominant design is the landmark event of an industry's life, and firms' survival depends on navigating it.**

The canonical case is the typewriter: dozens of entrants between 1885 and 1915, many of them renegades from Remington and Caligraph — then Underwood's **Model 5** (1899) bundled the QWERTY keyboard, visible type, the tab feature, shift-key capitalization, and the carriage cylinder into one machine and became the standard for decades. The same pattern recurs across autos (Dodge's all-steel closed body), TV picture tubes (RCA's 21-inch color tube, 1958), transistors (the planar transistor, 1959), electronic calculators, and glass (the float process). The managerial punchline: a dominant design is usually recognized only **in retrospect**, and firms fail mainly because they **cannot change their organization and practices in tandem with the evolution of technology**.

#### Key Concepts & Vocabulary

- **Dominant design** — "the one design in a product class that wins the allegiance of the marketplace — the one that competitors and innovators must adhere to if they hope to command significant market following." Out loud: *"the standard the market locks onto, usually a synthesis of features already proven separately in earlier variants."* Crucially, it is **not necessarily the technically best** design: it is a *satisficer* of many users' needs, not an optimizer for a few.
- **Fluid phase** — the early era of an industry: many entrants, rapid product experimentation, no settled standards, customers still learning what they want. Competition is about *product* novelty.
- **Transitional phase** — the period around the emergence of the dominant design, when the market begins to converge on one architecture.
- **Specific phase** — the era after the dominant design: products are standardized, competition shifts to *process* innovation — cost, quality, and scale of production.
- **Product innovation vs. process innovation** — product innovation = new designs, features, architectures; process innovation = new ways of *making* things (cheaper, faster, better quality). Utterback's signature finding: the **rate of product innovation peaks before the dominant design; the rate of process innovation peaks after it**.
- **Shakeout / exit wave** — the sharp decline in the number of firms after a dominant design appears, as competition shifts to scale and cost.
- **Assembled vs. nonassembled products** — assembled products (cars, typewriters, TVs) combine many components → the dominant *product* design is the landmark event. Nonassembled products (glass, rayon, steel, pulp & paper, industrial gases) flow through continuous processes → the dominant *process* design matters more (e.g., the float-glass process).
- **Collateral assets** (Teece) — complementary assets a firm already owns: brand, distribution channels, service networks, customer switching costs. Owning them helps a firm push *its* design to dominance (the IBM PC example).
- **Strategic maneuvering** (Cusumano, Mylonadis & Rosenbloom) — firm strategy decides which design wins: VHS beat the technically superior Betamax because JVC built alliances ("humble" strategy) while Sony went it alone.
- **Lead users / producer–user communication** (von Hippel) — close contact with early, sophisticated users teaches firms which product features truly matter before the design freezes.
- **Creative destruction** (Schumpeter) — a successful innovation creates a temporary monopoly with high margins, which attracts imitators and entrants, which erodes that monopoly — the engine of industrial dynamics.
- **Technological trajectory / design hierarchy** (Kim Clark) — once a core technical concept is chosen, later design decisions are constrained along a path; the QWERTY keyboard locked keyboard evolution onto one trajectory.
- **Lock-in via complementary assets** — QWERTY persisted partly because users and firms had invested in typing skills and training; established practice has momentum of its own.
- **Enabling technology** (nonassembled products) — a process technology that incorporates many needed elements and lets the industry shift from process *innovation* to process *improvement* (float glass).

#### Core Mechanics / Theories

**1. The fluid stage — why entry explodes.** In a young assembled-product industry no firm has a lock: no perfected products, no mastered manufacturing processes, no unassailable control of distribution, and customers have not yet formed an idea of the "ideal" product. As long as capital and technical barriers are low, entry is easy — with typewriters in the late 1800s "a good hand with mechanical gadgets" was enough, and Remington handled the capital-intensive manufacturing. Everyone — producers *and* customers — is experimenting and learning.

**2. What a dominant design is and what it does.** Definition above; note it usually *synthesizes* features introduced independently in prior variants (the Model 5 did not invent QWERTY or visible type — it combined proven elements). Three consequences:
- **It makes performance requirements implicit.** Nobody asks anymore whether a car has an electric starter or windshield wipers, whether a typewriter does upper- and lowercase, or whether a PC has a disk drive — these once-unique features become taken-for-granted parts of the accepted design.
- **It enforces standardization**, so competition shifts from design novelty to **cost, scale, and product performance**.
- **It redirects and slows innovation.** QWERTY put keyboard redesign off-limits (only peripheral keys could change). Firms stop tinkering with the product — "you don't take a well person to the surgeon" — and pour energy into process improvements.

**3. How dominant designs occur — they are NOT predetermined.** It would be tempting to think the internal-combustion car was "meant to be." Utterback's answer: a dominant design is "the result of the interplay between technical and market choices at any particular time." Four forces (plus inertia):
- **Collateral assets** — IBM's 1981 PC entry: brand value, expected service and parts, and the software ecosystem that would form around its standard.
- **Industry regulation & government** — the FCC's approval of RCA's television broadcast standard effectively made RCA's design the TV standard; governments today jockey over HDTV standards.
- **Strategic maneuvering** — the VHS vs. Betamax story: JVC's alliance-building "humble" strategy beat Sony's go-it-alone approach even though VHS was technically inferior.
- **Communication between producers and users** — staying close to customers shows firms which features truly matter; Christensen adds that entrenched customers can also pressure firms *not* to change when change is needed.
- **Inertia & complementary assets** — QWERTY's persistence reflects the momentum of established practice (typing skills, training).

**4. The life-cycle signature: entry → peak → exit.** Following Klein (each firm's investment is an experiment), the number of firms rises fast during experimentation, peaks around the dominant design, then exits surge and the count falls until a few large firms remain — very often **early entrants**. This entry/exit curve is the empirical fingerprint of dominant-design theory.

**5. The evidence — numbers you must be able to quote.**

| Industry | Dominant design (year) | Key numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Typewriters | Underwood **Model 5** (1899): QWERTY + visible type + tab + shift key + carriage cylinder | 4 competitors broke Remington's monopoly by 1885; **30+ firms by 1900**; Royal entered 1904; **40 firms by 1909**; then consolidation |
| Automobiles | **All-steel closed body** (Dodge, 1923) | **100+ firms** entered early US auto industry (steam, electric, ICE variants); by 1925 half of US production was all-steel closed-body; by 1940 **only five producers** had standing |
| TV picture tubes | **RCA 21-inch all-glass color tube** (announced May 1958) | Held a large share of the color-tube market; fostered concentration in the late 1950s |
| Transistors | **Planar transistor** (commercial production ~1959) | Entry surged after the 1948 invention and **virtually halted ~1959**; exits, almost nil before 1959, became commonplace after |
| Electronic calculators | Electronic (IC-based) calculator | Early 1960s: **five** major *electromechanical* firms; most failed the transition to electronics |
| Glass (nonassembled) | **Float-glass process** (process dominant design) | For nonassembled products the process, not the product, is the main battlefield |
| Winchester disk drives | IBM's 1973 technology, then standardization | Many entrants, then shakeout; production-volume capabilities became an entry barrier |

**The transistor story in detail (the sharpest example of incumbent failure).** In 1950, General Electric, Philco Ford, and RCA dominated the *vacuum tube* business — and over the next decade they spent more on R&D and received more patents than new entrants did. Yet a cohort of **new entrants captured nearly two-thirds of the new transistor market**; by 1966, **Texas Instruments, Motorola, and Fairchild together held 42%**, while the vacuum-tube makers' share of the transistor market fell to just over one-fourth. Lesson: incumbents' money and patents did not save them from a competence-destroying technology.

**6. Assembled vs. nonassembled products.** For nonassembled products (rayon, glass, pulp & paper, metals, industrial gases) — fewer materials, more concentrated experimentation in the *process* — innovation in the production process, not the emergence of a dominant product design, is the more powerful theme. A process "enabling technology" lets the industry shift from process innovation to process improvement (float glass). Teece's caveat (footnote 1 of the chapter): the dominant-design model suits **mass markets with relatively homogeneous tastes** and extends only to **assembled** manufacturing industries.

**7. Can we recognize a dominant design when we see it?** Only **in retrospect**. Simplicity and elegance characterize many dominant designs (the DC-3 aircraft), but trying to predict one by mapping product features alone is doomed to frustration. Yet the inability to spot it early does not diminish its impact — it still reorganizes innovation and competition.

**8. Survival of firms — the organizational-failure argument.** The chapter's most quotable sentence: **the inability of firms to change organizational structure and practices in tandem with the evolution of technology is a major source of failure.** Incumbents usually win the process/scale game but often cannot switch to a new product logic (vacuum tubes → transistors; electromechanical → electronic calculators); entrants often win the new design but die later in the scale game. Survival belongs to firms that can do both — which is rare.

#### Literature Synthesis

This module *is* the assigned reading for L1 (Utterback, 1996, Ch. 2, pp. 23–56). Here is the citable "data bank" — the specific arguments to invoke, and what each supports:

- **Fluid-stage description (pp. 23–25):** no firm has a lock on the market; producers and customers co-experiment. *Supports:* why entry explodes in new industries — the mechanism behind industrial dynamics.
- **Model 5 evidence (pp. 25–26):** Underwood's Model 5 (1899) synthesized QWERTY, visible type, tab, shift-key capitalization, carriage cylinder; copycats (Remington Monarch 1900; L.C. Smith Brothers) trailed Underwood's sales. *Supports:* dominant design = synthesis of proven features, not a radical invention; the IBM PC repeated the same pattern decades later (TV monitor + standard processor + MS-DOS, little breakthrough technology).
- **QWERTY persistence (pp. 27–28, 31):** hundreds of keyboard designs were possible; QWERTY locked in through established practice and complementary assets (typing skills). *Supports:* non-predetermination + lock-in; "technological trajectory" via Kim Clark's design hierarchies.
- **Four emergence forces (pp. 28–30):** collateral assets (IBM PC, 1981); regulation (FCC/RCA TV); strategic maneuvering (Cusumano et al. on VHS vs. Betamax — JVC's alliance strategy beat Sony's go-it-alone); producer–user communication (von Hippel's lead users; Christensen's resistant customers). *Supports:* a dominant design is the interplay of technical *and* market choices.
- **Entry/exit numbers:** typewriters (4 competitors by 1885; 30+ by 1900; 40 by 1909); autos (100+ entrants; five producers by 1940); transistors (entry halted ~1959 with the planar transistor; TI/Motorola/Fairchild = 42% by 1966 vs. tube makers ~25%); calculators (five electromechanical firms → electronic). *Supports:* the shakeout signature — firm numbers peak around the dominant design.
- **Klein's experiments view:** each firm's investments and product introductions are experiments generating corrective feedback. *Supports:* why shakeout follows the dominant design.
- **Schumpeter's creative destruction:** innovation → temporary monopoly → high margins → entrants → erosion. *Supports:* the supply-side engine linking innovation to industrial dynamics.
- **Nonassembled products & Teece's caveat (pp. 48–50 and fn. 1):** for glass, rayon, etc., process innovation dominates; the model fits mass markets and assembled industries. *Supports:* knowing the *boundary conditions* of the theory — a favorite examiner test.
- **Retrospect problem (pp. 50–51):** dominant designs are recognizable only after the fact; simplicity/elegance (DC-3) is characteristic but not predictive. *Supports:* humility about prediction — bridges to Bresnahan's argument that "GPT-ness" is also an ex-post property.
- **Organizational failure (p. 30):** firms fail by not changing organization and practices in tandem with technology. *Supports:* the survival question in the chapter's title — and the management takeaway.

**Connection to the course's Swedish angle:** the ILO asks you to apply such concepts to industrial development in Sweden. Practice this now: Sweden's steel industry (see L2 reading, Karakaya et al. 2018) faces exactly Utterback's dilemma — a hydrogen-based process could be a *process* dominant-design shift in a nonassembled industry, where incumbents' scale assets can help or blind them.

#### Oral Exam Interrogation Prep

**Q1. "Define a dominant design in one sentence, and give me an example with numbers."**
*Speaking points:* "A dominant design is the one product architecture that wins the allegiance of the marketplace — competitors must adhere to it to command significant market share. Example: Underwood's Model 5 typewriter (1899) combined QWERTY, visible type, tab, shift-key capitalization, and a carriage cylinder into one machine; the market locked onto it, copycats like the Remington Monarch (1900) trailed Underwood's sales, and the design held for decades. The firm population tells the same story: 30+ firms by 1900, 40 by 1909 — then consolidation."
*Pushback:* "But VHS beat Betamax even though VHS was technically inferior — doesn't that contradict the idea of a 'best' design?" — No, it confirms Utterback: a dominant design is a *satisficer*, not a technical optimizer. JVC won by strategic maneuvering (alliances, licensing) while Sony went it alone. Dominance is won in the marketplace, not in the lab.

**Q2. "Walk me through what happens to innovation and competition before and after a dominant design emerges."**
*Speaking points:* Before: fluid phase — many entrants, product experimentation, customers learn what they want; competition on design novelty. At the landmark event: the design makes many performance requirements *implicit* (electric starters, disk drives are no longer selling points). After: product innovation slows, process innovation accelerates — competition shifts to cost, scale, and product performance; standardization lets complementary economies be pursued; exits surge (shakeout) and a few large firms remain, often early entrants. Quote the typewriter/transistor numbers as evidence.
*Pushback:* "Why do firms exit right after the design appears — why can't they just copy it?" — Because the competitive game changes: winners are those who can scale production and drive down cost, not those with clever designs. Copying the design is necessary but not sufficient; firms that can't convert to process competition and organizational practices suited to it are forced out.

**Q3. "Why did new entrants beat incumbents in transistors, when incumbents outspent them on R&D?"**
*Speaking points:* GE, Philco Ford, and RCA dominated vacuum tubes in 1950 and earned more patents than entrants — yet entrants captured ~2/3 of the new transistor market, and by 1966 TI, Motorola, and Fairchild held 42% versus ~25% for the tube makers. Because the transistor was competence-destroying for tube firms: their organizational structures, skills, and incentives were built around tube process technology, and they could not change organization and practices in tandem with the new technology — Utterback's central failure mechanism.
*Pushback:* "So first-mover advantage doesn't exist?" — It exists, but it is conditional: early entrants benefit when they enter in the fluid phase and ride the dominant design to scale; incumbents benefit when the technology is competence-enhancing (e.g., incremental, process-related). The survival question is about *matching* capabilities to the phase of industry evolution.

**Q4. "Can a manager recognize a dominant design while it is happening?"**
*Speaking points:* Utterback's honest answer: only in retrospect. Simplicity and elegance are characteristic (the DC-3), but mapping features cannot predict the winner — it emerges from the interplay of technical and market choices, including collateral assets, regulation, strategy, and producer–user communication. Managers should manage the *process* (stay close to lead users, build collateral assets, choose alliance strategies) rather than pretend to predict the design.
*Pushback:* "If it's only visible in retrospect, is the concept useful?" — Yes: even unrecognized in real time, the design reorganizes competition in predictable ways — after it lands, competition shifts to process/cost and shakeout follows. The theory gives managers a *conditional strategy*: know which phase your industry is in, and match innovation investments accordingly.

**Q5. "The model fits assembled products and mass markets. What happens in nonassembled industries like glass?"**
*Speaking points:* For nonassembled products (rayon, glass, pulp & paper, metals, industrial gases), the dominant design is a *process* design: experimentation concentrates in the production process, and an "enabling technology" (e.g., the float-glass process) lets the industry shift from process innovation to process improvement and scale. Teece's caveat: the product dominant-design model suits mass markets with homogeneous tastes and assembled manufacturing industries.
*Pushback:* "Then is the dominant design concept wrong for process industries?" — Not wrong, but relocated: the landmark event and the shakeout logic still apply, just at the level of the production process rather than the product. The competition shift — from innovation to cost and scale — is the same.

### Module 1B — What Innovation Paths for AI to Become a GPT? (Bresnahan, 2024)

*Source: T. Bresnahan, "What innovation paths for AI to become a GPT?" Journal of Economics & Management Strategy 33(2), 2024, pp. 305–316 (received 2022, accepted 2023). Second assigned reading for L1.*

#### TL;DR

Modern AI technologies (AITs) could become **general-purpose technologies (GPTs)** — the kind of technology, like electricity or ICT, that lifts the whole economy. But their early commercial applications (roughly 2015–2017), while enormously profitable, are **narrow but deep**: recommendation systems, advertising targeting, and feed curation that run the core production processes of the internet giants — firms with sales in the hundreds of billions. Bresnahan's question: *what innovation paths could widen that narrow range into economy-wide, growth-boosting use?* His answer in one sentence: a **GPT loop** — positive feedback between GPT improvements and **application-sector coinvention** — requires real *invention* on the application side, and that invention is not automatic.

The paper's striking empirical claim: early AI success happened exactly **where Digital Transformation (DT) was NOT required** — the internet giants were already digital, data-based, and algorithmic. Every candidate path to generality (imitating the giants, automating jobs, voice interfaces, spreading prediction) turns out to **require Digital Transformation and organizational change** as key steps — and technical progress in AI has *not* made that transformation cheaper. The bottlenecks remain in **coinvention**, so the most likely paths are slow, indirect, and organizational rather than purely technical. This is the analytical spine of the course's "AI-driven transformation" challenge.

#### Key Concepts & Vocabulary

- **AI technologies (AITs)** — the modern wave of AI: machine-learning systems whose core is **statistical prediction** (predicting the next click, word, or pixel), not general "thinking." Say it as: "AITs are prediction machines, not workers."
- **General-purpose technology (GPT)** — the ex-post definition has three properties: (1) **pervasive use** throughout the economy; (2) **continuous improvement** over time; (3) **innovational complementarities** — it enables/spawns complementary inventions in application sectors. Examples: steam, electricity, ICT.
- **GPT loop** — the positive feedback cycle: improvements in the GPT enable valuable applications (coinvention); those applications generate demand, data, and revenue that fund and guide further GPT improvements. A loop, not a one-shot invention.
- **Application sector (AS) coinvention** — the complementary inventions that must happen *in the sectors using the technology*: new organizational structures, new workflows, new customer relationships, new processes. This is Bresnahan's central bottleneck: **coinvention, not the core technology, is scarce.**
- **Digital Transformation (DT)** — converting a firm's processes, data, and organization to digital, data-based operation. The paper's key finding: early AI wins happened where DT was *already done* (the internet giants); every path to broader AI use requires DT first.
- **Internet giants (IGs)** — Google, Amazon, Meta/Facebook, Netflix et al.: already-digital firms whose modular, data-rich production systems made AI application invention cheap.
- **Recipe approaches** — lazy arguments that AI will become a GPT simply because it is powerful and improving ("everything big grew after slow growth"). Bresnahan's critique: recipes must forecast *all three* GPT properties — pervasiveness and complementarity cannot be assumed from technical power alone.
- **Anthropomorphic error** — the false belief that an AI system is "like a worker" that can replace a human one-for-one. AITs have substituted for *other capital* or done tasks previously not done — not replaced labor to any significant degree.
- **Automation vs. augmentation** — automation = replacing human work; augmentation = making human work better (decision support, driver assistance). The market switched from the automation framing to augmentation after the automation pitch failed with enterprise customers.
- **Organizational adjustment costs (OAC)** — the costs of changing an organization to use a technology. High OAC explains why AI spreads slowly beyond already-digital firms.
- **Inventive visibility** — how easy it is to foresee a valuable application before building it. Low visibility (hard-to-foresee uses) slows diffusion.
- **Capital–capital complementarity** — AI complements *other capital* (data infrastructure, cloud, software) rather than substituting labor. AITs "increase the depth and flexibility of the ICT stack."
- **Technology stack** — the layered set of general-purpose components (chips, cloud, data, prediction) that applications combine. Raises the question: *what exactly is "the GPT"?*
- **Technological determinism** — the error of thinking "being a GPT" is a property of the technology alone. Bresnahan: it is also a property of application sectors and their capacity to coinvent.
- **Recombinatory invention / indirect path** — the historical pattern (computer arithmetic → business data processing → enterprise computing) where a capability becomes general only through a long chain of application inventions.

#### Core Mechanics / Theories

**1. The puzzle: narrow but deep.** The first commercial AIT applications (2015–2017) were extremely valuable but confined to a narrow range: they run the *core production processes* of internet giants with sales in the hundreds of billions. If comparable applications diffused across the economy, the result would be a productivity "growth boom" and huge strategic gains for emulators. So the stakes are enormous — but "narrow but deep" is the empirical starting point. Note the sharp observation: academics see "AI everywhere" (technical users in science and engineering adopted fast), but **the large commercial gains arise in just a few firms in a few industries**.

**2. Why the internet giants won first.** Their existing digital production systems **lowered the cost of application invention**: modular, data-based, algorithmic processes meant AI (prediction) slotted into an already-digital stack. This is the key inversion: early AI use succeeded **precisely where Digital Transformation was not required**, because DT had already happened. Firms that have not yet gone through DT use AI only for incremental value; firms already through DT (the IGs) capture the big gains.

**3. The GPT loop and why "recipe" thinking fails.** A GPT loop is positive feedback: GPT improvements enable application-sector coinvention; applications create demand/data/revenue that drive further GPT improvements. **Invention in both the GPT and the application sector starts the loop, expands its scope, and builds momentum** — the process is not automatic. "Recipe" approaches (AI is powerful and improving, therefore it will become general) must forecast all three ex-post GPT properties (pervasiveness, improvement, innovational complementarities) and typically have no basis for the second and third. The historical lesson: *improvements in the underlying GPT are necessary but not sufficient* — brilliant AIT inventions matter less than brilliant **application** inventions.

**4. The path audit — what could widen AI use, and where each path stands.**

| Path | Mechanism | Status per Bresnahan |
|---|---|---|
| **IG imitation** | Other firms emulate the giants' AI use (recommendation, targeting, matching) | Stalled. Surveys 2016–2019: >40% of enterprises experimenting (peaking near half), but very low deployment rates — a higher failure rate than usual for radical ICT. Senior *business* people pushed experiments on buzz ("get me an AI"), not on overlap between business needs and technical capabilities. |
| **Job automation** | Replace workers task-by-task; common jobs → quick generality | The metaphor is false. AITs have not substituted for human labor to any significant degree — they substitute for other capital (as at the IGs) or do tasks previously not done at all. "An AI" is an anthropomorphic error. |
| **Diffusion of a successful coinvention** | Copy matching engines (as invented at the IGs) across sectors | Real but heterogeneous: value depends on the adopting firm's DT maturity. An IG executive's contrast: his own modular production vs. firms asking for "salesmen who could build us a customized FAQ." |
| **Augmentation switch** | AI improves human work instead of replacing it | Working. Expert systems became decision-support (underwriting, diagnosis); "driverless vehicle" tech was rapidly repurposed as driver-assist features (repurposed components + high inventive visibility + EU regulation). Incremental at the single-worker level, not organizational. |
| **Voice UIs / NLP** | Natural-language interfaces (Alexa, search, enterprise commands) | Promising but bounded: valuable where hands are busy or safety matters (kitchens, cars, healthcare). Near term more like **touch screens** than like the Internet — an access technology. |
| **Robots & programmer tools** | MV in robots; automated coding/debugging | Smaller trends. Programming: testing/debugging is >half of programmers' work, so real gains are plausible — consistent with ICT succeeding fastest among technical users. |
| **Prediction-core path** | Prediction (Agrawal, Gans & Goldfarb) as the GPT that draws complements and breaks out of prediction-shaped uses | Possible but slow. The arithmetic analogy: computing "arithmetic" became general only via business data processing — a card-tabulator-compatible, low-OAC, incrementally visible step — and then a decades-long, indirect path. The bottleneck is on the *use* side: data technologies exist, but intelligent use of data remains rare in enterprises. |

**5. Reframing the GPT discussion — two problems.**

- **Problem 1: what is "the GPT" when there is a technology stack?** Applications combine many general-purpose components (chips, cloud, data, prediction) in varying proportions. Two valid answers: (a) *the whole stack* is the GPT and a new layer is just an improvement; or (b) *each general-purpose component with a positive feedback loop* is its own GPT. Because AI prediction is so tightly complementary with other ICT — especially data technologies — there may be no analytical purpose in treating AITs as a separate GPT at all. The observed pattern is **capital–capital complementarity**: AITs increase the depth and flexibility of the existing ICT stack rather than acting as a stand-alone revolutionary technology.
- **Problem 2: technological determinism.** It is an error to think "being a GPT" is an attribute only of a technology, not of application sectors. History: factory electrification (Du Boff), machine tools (Rosenberg), computer programming — generality arrived through *invented applications*, often organizational. The bottlenecks to launching a GPT loop for past ICT were in inventing key applications — new organizational structures, new customer relationships — and those paths are slow.

**6. Conclusion — the bottlenecks remain in coinvention.** AIT use in enterprise systems is **narrow but deep**; the big gains sit in a few firms and industries. *All* promising paths require not only better AITs but **widespread Digital Transformation** — and technical progress has not lowered the invention costs that make DT difficult and scarce. The paper's closing judgment: **brilliant AIT inventions or large public investments in AITs are likely to be less efficacious than brilliant application inventions in moving the whole loop forward.** Expect slow, indirect paths; the most profitable firm-level route may be data-technology investment now, AI deployment much later. (Bresnahan explicitly sets aside other serious issues — surveillance, bias from backward-looking training data, the risk of AI research migrating from open science to a single provider.)

#### Literature Synthesis

This module *is* the second assigned reading for L1. Citable evidence and its role:

- **Survey evidence for the stalled IG-imitation path:** enterprise surveys 2016–2019 report high experimentation (over 40% of enterprises, peaking at almost half) but persistently **low deployment** of AIT-based systems — a higher failure rate than the usual high rate for radical ICT projects. Crucially, the push came from **senior business people**, driven by buzz rather than by an assessment of the overlap between business needs and technical capabilities — the "get me an AI" phenomenon. *Use this whenever asked why AI has not diffused faster.*
- **The automation axiom, quoted:** "At the heart of our framework is the idea that automation and thus AI and robotics replace workers in tasks that they previously performed" (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018, p. 198). Bresnahan: the metaphor is false — AITs substitute for capital or do previously undone tasks; the "driverless vehicle" hype revitalized this old mistake.
- **The augmentation switch:** expert systems, once sold as decision *substitutes*, converted easily to decision *support* (underwriting, diagnosis); driverless-vehicle components were rapidly repurposed into driver-assist features, which sell well — nearly all new EU vehicles have some (regulation helped). *Shows ex-post market selection working: technologies often succeed in a form different from the pitch.*
- **Voice/NLP evidence:** voice UIs are valuable in kitchens, cars (inconvenient/dangerous for traditional UIs) and healthcare (safety); industries ranked by voice recognition are all in health care provision. Near term, voice is more like touch screens than like the Internet.
- **The arithmetic analogy (the deepest historical argument):** computer arithmetic became general through **business data processing** — the key recombinatory invention was a computer compatible with existing punched-card data storage/retrieval/I-O, permitting incremental improvements ("what if a card tabulator could do more arithmetic?"): low organizational adjustment costs, localized visibility. Then came decades of slow, indirect application invention; arithmetic ended up coequal with data storage and transmission, not the center of its own loop. *Moral: expect AI's generality to arrive through equally slow, organizational, use-side inventions — if at all.*
- **"AI everywhere" among technical users:** predictive AITs have already spread fast in science and engineering (Cockburn et al., 2018) — but technical-user diffusion does not equal commercial generality (Bresnahan & Malerba's distinction). *Explains why hype and reality diverge.*
- **The indirect-path prediction:** AITs have been easiest to adopt where applications were **already data-based and algorithmic**; long-run benefits may arrive indirectly — future investment in data technologies, followed much later by AIT deployment.

**Weaving into the course's practice layer:** the guest lectures illustrate Bresnahan's application-side bottleneck in the wild — AI for renewable energy (prediction was already familiar: forecasting load/weather), AI for clinics (voice and decision support where safety matters — exactly the healthcare pattern Bresnahan notes), LLMs in gaming (an already-digital, data-rich industry, i.e., a natural "already transformed" adopter). The food-sector lecture (One Planet Plate) is the mirror image: a sustainability transition where the bottleneck is changing practices and demand, not technology. Use these as ready-made examples.

#### Oral Exam Interrogation Prep

**Q1. "What would it take for AI to become a general-purpose technology, according to Bresnahan?"**
*Speaking points:* Three things have to be true at once: pervasive use, continuous improvement, and innovational complementarities — applications sectors must invent complementary uses. The mechanism is a **GPT loop**: GPT improvements enable application coinvention; applications create demand, data, and revenue that drive further improvements. The loop must be *started* by invention on both sides — it is not automatic. Bresnahan's twist: the binding constraint is not the AI technology but **application-sector coinvention** — organizational structures, workflows, customer relationships — plus the **Digital Transformation** that must precede it.
*Pushback:* "AI is already everywhere — hasn't it become a GPT?" — Distinguish technical users from commercial breadth: AI has spread fast in science, engineering, and the internet giants, but enterprise use remains **narrow but deep** — the big commercial gains sit in a few firms and a few industries. "Everywhere among academics" is not the same as economy-wide generality.

**Q2. "Why did the first commercial AI successes happen at the internet giants?"**
*Speaking points:* Because their production systems were **already digital, data-based, and algorithmic** — AI prediction slotted into an existing stack, so application invention was cheap. That is the paper's striking inversion: early AI succeeded exactly where **Digital Transformation was NOT required**, because it had already happened. Firms that haven't transformed get only incremental value from the same tools.
*Pushback:* "So other firms can just imitate the giants?" — The IG-imitation path is precisely the one that stalled: surveys show over 40% of enterprises experimenting but very low deployment, with projects pushed by senior managers on buzz rather than on a real overlap between needs and capabilities ("get me an AI"). Imitation requires the hard part — DT — which the recipe for imitation leaves out.

**Q3. "Bresnahan says 'the automation metaphor is false.' Defend or attack that claim."**
*Speaking points:* Defend it empirically: AITs have not substituted for human labor to any significant degree — they have substituted for **other capital** (as at the internet giants) or performed tasks previously not done at all. The idea that "an AI" replaces a worker one-for-one is an **anthropomorphic error** — AITs are prediction machines, not workers. Jobs are bundles of heterogeneous tasks embedded in organizations; replacing one task rarely removes a job, and the reorganization costs (OAC) are what slow everything down.
*Pushback:* "But generative AI now writes text and code that humans used to write — doesn't that falsify the claim?" — First, note the paper predates the LLM wave, but its logic extends: the question is whether the *task-level* substitution adds up to job-level replacement without massive organizational change. Second, Bresnahan's evidence for the "augmentation switch" (expert systems → decision support; driverless → driver-assist) suggests the pattern will repeat: technologies sell as replacements and succeed as augmentations. The burden of proof for job-level automation remains unmet.

**Q4. "What is coinvention, and why is it the bottleneck?"**
*Speaking points:* Coinvention = the complementary inventions that application sectors must make to use a general technology profitably: new organizational structures, processes, relationships with customers. It is the bottleneck because **technical progress in AI has not lowered the invention costs that make Digital Transformation difficult and scarce**. History repeats this: electricity needed factory redesign (Du Boff), machine tools needed application invention (Rosenberg), computing needed business data processing. Bresnahan's bottom line: brilliant AIT inventions matter less than brilliant *application* inventions.
*Pushback:* "If AI is so powerful, why wouldn't Digital Transformation get cheaper automatically?" — Because DT is an *organizational* invention, not a computational one. The arithmetic analogy: computing became general only via a long chain of use-side inventions, and arithmetic ended up coequal with data handling rather than its own center. The bottleneck sits on the use side — and no amount of model improvement removes it.

**Q5. "Bresnahan reframes the GPT discussion with two problems. Explain both, and why they matter."**
*Speaking points:* Problem 1 — **the stack**: applications combine many general-purpose components, so "the GPT" is ambiguous: either the whole stack is the GPT, or each feedback-loop component is. AI prediction is so tightly complementary with data and cloud technologies (capital–capital complementarity) that treating it as a separate GPT may have no analytical purpose. Problem 2 — **technological determinism**: being a GPT is not an attribute of the technology alone; application sectors' capacity to coinvent decides. Both matter because they redirect policy and strategy: invest in applications and organizational change, not just in bigger models.
*Pushback:* "Isn't this just definitional nitpicking?" — No: the definitions carry predictions. If the stack is the GPT, then AI's value arrives through upgrades to existing systems (incremental, complements) — which matches the evidence. If GPT-ness is deterministic, we expect rapid, automatic diffusion — which the survey evidence contradicts. The reframing explains *why* AI's diffusion has stalled, which is an empirical question, not a semantic one.

### Module 1C — Synthesis: Dominant Design × GPT Loops (the L1 integration)

Examiners love cross-cutting questions. Here is the ready-made argument connecting the two L1 readings:

**Where the two theories agree.** Both reject technological determinism and locate the outcome in *market and organizational choices*, not in the technology's raw power:
- Utterback: a dominant design "is not necessarily predetermined, but is the result of the interplay between technical and market choices" — and it is recognizable only in retrospect.
- Bresnahan: "being a GPT is an attribute not only of a technology, but of application sectors" — and GPT-ness is an ex-post property that "recipes" cannot forecast.
- Both see **complementary assets/coinvention** as decisive: Utterback's collateral assets (brand, channels, switching costs) are the within-industry twin of Bresnahan's cross-sector coinvention (organizations, workflows, customer relationships). Teece's footnote and Bresnahan's Problem 2 say the same thing from different levels.

**Where they differ — and why the pairing is powerful.** Utterback describes a *within-industry life cycle*: fluid → dominant design → shakeout → process competition. Bresnahan describes a *cross-economy diffusion loop*: GPT improvement ↔ application coinvention. One stabilizes an industry; the other destabilizes many sectors. Together: **L1's thesis is that industrial dynamics are driven by design convergence inside industries and by general-purpose technologies across them.**

**Two synthesis claims you can make in the exam (pick one and defend it):**
1. *"Has AI produced a dominant design yet?"* Defensible answer: **no** — AI application markets are still in Utterback's fluid phase: abundant experimentation, low deployment, no settled architecture of use; the "design" that dominates is the *existing* ICT stack itself, which is exactly Bresnahan's capital–capital complementarity argument.
2. *"Is the AI stack the GPT?"* Bresnahan's Problem 1 reframed in Utterback's language: the stack (chips + cloud + data + prediction) is the dominant design of the digital era, and new AI layers succeed by becoming **transparent to users** — just as Utterback says successful PC innovations "more often than not are transparent to users" (QWERTY, disk drives, operating systems are features users refuse to have meddled with).

**Swedish application (practice for ILO 1):** ask "where is Sweden's economy in these frameworks?" Steel (a nonassembled, process industry) faces a possible hydrogen-based process dominant design — an incumbent/process-innovation battleground in Utterback's terms. Swedish firms adopting AI face Bresnahan's DT-and-coinvention bottleneck, not a shortage of models. These two sentences connect L1 to the L2 reading (Karakaya et al. 2018) and to the course's two societal challenges.
